# International seekers: evidence, differences and research plan

29 September 2026. **PROPOSED research synthesis.** The founder has expanded the long-term audience beyond India. That is an explicit direction, not evidence of international demand. Existing family-introduction and younger self-use audiences remain included. No interviews, paid-demand tests or account Insights were collected for this report.

## What the available evidence supports

The international audience is not one secular Western persona. Pew's cross-national research documents substantial differences in religious/spiritual practice between societies. Its measures do not identify prospective Shanivar customers or willingness to use Indian contemplative material. Treat geography, religion, language, prior practice and willingness to investigate as separate variables. [Pew, 36-country practices report](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/06/spiritual-and-religious-practices/).

Pew's 2023 US survey used 11,201 adults and distinguishes overlapping spiritual/religious identities. It supports asking people how they describe themselves rather than assigning identity from a meditation interest. It cannot establish a global market or imply that nonreligious people reject all tradition. [Survey and methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/religious-landscape-study/2023/12/07/spirituality-among-americans/).

Park and colleagues studied 360 yoga students and 156 teachers. They report different starting and continuing motivations, with spirituality more prominent in some accounts of continued practice. This was retrospective self-report among practitioners, not a longitudinal conversion trajectory or a sample of people who quit. **Inference to test:** offer depth when requested instead of assuming either permanent casual use or inevitable spiritual progression. [Author-hosted paper, methods/results](https://spiritualitymeaningandhealth.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2598/2019/03/Why-practice-yoga.pdf).

Hunt and colleagues' US online sample of 621 supported four perceived-barrier dimensions: benefit, knowledge, practical obstacles and sociocultural conflict. These are useful interview topics, not a diagnosis or a mandatory intake score. The sample does not validate the inventory internationally. [DMPI-R study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33664878/).

Xie and colleagues interviewed 17 people after a four-week app intervention. Their accounts identify integration barriers and possible facilitators for informal practice. Small, selected, post-intervention interviews can suggest wording and situations; they cannot establish that reminders, personalization or ten-second practices cause lasting benefit. Some authors have Healthy Minds affiliations. [Study abstract and disclosures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39070019/).

## Cultural translation and authority

There is a real tension between removing religious language to lower entry friction and erasing the people and traditions behind a practice. Arundhati Baitmangalkar writes as an Indian immigrant teacher about acknowledgment, context and respectful engagement. This is a situated practitioner account, not population consensus. [Her essay](https://yogainternational.com/article/view/how-we-can-work-together-to-avoid-cultural-appropriation-in-yoga/).

The Hindu American Foundation explicitly challenges the omission of Hindu roots in commercial yoga descriptions. Record that as an advocacy position, not an impartial history or permission to relabel Buddhist, Jain or every tantric tradition as one Hindu school. [HAF's account of its campaign](https://www.hinduamerican.org/projects/hindu-roots-of-yoga).

**Proposed Shanivar response:** plain action first; a visible short provenance label; optional source, translator, tradition, adaptation and disagreement behind it. Pay appropriate reviewers for work rather than using an ornamental Sanskrit word as proof of legitimacy. Credit living interpretive contributors without implying their endorsement. Ask practitioners what has been omitted or misrepresented. A cultural advisory review should include differing Indian and diaspora perspectives, not one person appointed to speak for India.

Guru skepticism should be investigated through concrete authority questions: who can correct the app, whether disagreement changes access, whether a user can decline a worldview, and whether a referral involves a financial relationship. Do not infer skepticism from nationality. A beginner can desire textual authenticity and reject spiritual hierarchy simultaneously.

## Questions we do not yet know

**Terminology:** recognition of “tantra” may be doctrinal, sexualized, vague or absent; recognition of “mindfulness” does not prove understanding of Buddhist context. Test unaided meaning before explaining a term. Do not publish assumed familiarity percentages.

**Language:** English is the current research and seed-content language, not the presumed global preference. Ask preferred language separately for navigation, spoken guidance, philosophical explanation and source reading. A bilingual person may choose differently for each. Test reviewed Hindi and a locally relevant language only when competent reviewers and participant demand support them; do not auto-translate sacred/source text into authoritative claims.

**Willingness to practice:** compare what people say with an observed voluntary attempt and a later report of a self-chosen return. Do not treat a timer, a saved post or a screen-time drop as direct observation of practice quality.

**Willingness to pay:** unknown. Research the last contemplative service or tool actually purchased, what value it delivered, why it was cancelled and whether price, credibility or time was the bottleneck. A proposed price conversation after demonstrated usefulness is weaker evidence than a real, transparently described paid pilot. No payment should be taken before an authorized offer exists.

**Community:** people may seek accountability, lineage belonging, companionship, learning or none of these. Test an optional small discussion after private practice; do not require profiles, public streaks or practice disclosure. No community launch is recommended by this desk research alone.

## Continuous research protocol

Owner: User Researcher with Sadhak Journey Researcher; Staff Manager controls evidence intake. “Continuous” means repeatable owner-triggered cycles, not a background agent secretly collecting posts.

Proposed discovery cycle: 18 consenting adults, purposively sampled across India, Indian diaspora and international non-Indian explorers, with six in each group. Within each, seek different practice experience and include people who stopped using contemplative apps. Geography and group sizes are practical quotas, not statistical representation. Offer language choice and accessible interview formats. Do not infer religion, trauma, caste or mental health from profiles.

Interview prompts: last real moment of automatic checking; current workaround; last practice attempted; how they decided whether a teacher or app was credible; a term they found confusing; what made them stop; what they would prefer left unexplained. Let participants decline personal history. Present product concepts only after the problem interview.

Then run a separate counterbalanced comprehension session: plain instruction alone versus the same instruction with a concise source label and optional explanation. Record time to first voluntary action, help requests, correct explanation of the source/adaptation distinction, and whether the person felt pressured to believe. Obtain distinct consent before recording or quoting; anonymize notes and set a deletion date. Compensation is independent of praise or completion.

Review after each six interviews; change only the next interview guide if evidence exposes a blind spot. Do not generalize percentages from these quotas. At 7 and 28 days, optional follow-up distinguishes useful independent practice, helpful continued use, disappointed departure and unknown departure. Preserve those as different outcomes.
