# Technique 11: Open Horizon

## Primary Source
- **Verse:** Vijñāna-bhairava-tantra, verse 60.
- **Translation cited:** Christopher Wallis ("Hareesh"), *A Translation of the Vijñāna-bhairava-tantra* (2017, rev. 2018, hareesh.org).
- **Cross-checked against:** An independently-worded rendering of the same verse found via secondary sourcing: "One should cast one's gaze on an open (stretch of) land devoid of trees, mountains, walls etc. When the state of mind is fixed there, then the fluctuating thoughts dissolve (by themselves)" — different translator's phrasing, same content and same verse number, giving genuine independent confirmation.
- **What the verse says:** Look out at a stretch of open, featureless land — no trees, no mountains, no walls, nothing to break up the view — and let the mind settle into that openness. The verse describes the result as a quieting of ordinary mental "churning."

## Essence
This is a close cousin of sky-gazing (Technique 05), but horizontal rather than vertical, and framed slightly differently in the source: less about the eyes resting on emptiness, and more about the *mind* taking on the quality of the space it's looking at — spreading out, rather than staying knotted around a single point of concern.

## Guided Session Script
*(Adaptation — modern secular framing. Approximately 4 minutes. Best done somewhere with a genuinely open view — a field, a beach, a wide street, a large parking lot at a quiet hour, or a window overlooking distance. A large, blank interior space can substitute if no outdoor view is available.)*

Find a spot where you can see as far and as open a stretch as possible — the further and more featureless, the better. Standing or sitting both work.

Let your gaze settle on the open space itself, rather than on any specific object within it. If there's a building or a tree in view, let your attention pass over it rather than landing on it — you're looking *at* the openness, not *for* something in it.

Take a few breaths here. Notice the sheer amount of space in front of you compared to how much space your usual field of attention actually occupies day to day.

Let your sense of "where my attention is" widen to match what you're looking at — instead of attention feeling like a narrow beam, let it feel more like it's spread across the whole width of what you see.

If a worry or a repetitive thought shows up, notice it, then let your attention widen back out to the whole horizon rather than narrowing back down around the thought.

Stay like this for a while, letting the width of the view and the width of your attention stay loosely matched.

Before you finish, take one breath while looking at the widest part of the view, then let your gaze come back to whatever's nearby and continue your day.

## Modern Adaptation Notes
- Dropped: naming the result as the cessation of "*vṛtti*" in the technical Yogic sense (the term Patañjali uses for the fluctuations of mind that yoga is traditionally defined as stilling). Replaced with the plain English "churning" or "repetitive thought," since the Sanskrit technical term carries a specific philosophical weight (from the *Yoga-sūtra*) that isn't necessary for the practice itself to work.
- Kept: the core instruction essentially intact — an open, featureless view, attention resting on the space itself rather than any object in it.
- Added (adaptation only): the explicit reframing of "widening attention to match the width of the view" — the verse states the *result* (mental churning becomes feeble) without specifying *how* the practitioner should hold their attention to get there; we supplied a concrete mechanism (deliberately matching attentional width to visual width) as one reasonable, testable way to operationalize the verse's instruction.
