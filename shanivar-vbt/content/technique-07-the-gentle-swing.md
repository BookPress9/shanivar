# Technique 07: The Gentle Swing

## Primary Source
- **Verse:** Vijñāna-bhairava-tantra, verse 83.
- **Translation cited:** Christopher Wallis ("Hareesh"), *A Translation of the Vijñāna-bhairava-tantra* (2017, rev. 2018, hareesh.org).
- **Cross-check status — RESOLVED by QA (2026-09-08):** Independently confirmed against Jaideva Singh's *Vijñānabhairava or Divine Consciousness: A Treasury of 112 Types of Yoga* (Motilal Banarsidass, 1979; full text via archive.org), which numbers this same practice **verse 83** — an exact match to Wallis's numbering — with matching content: "O goddess, owing to the swinging of the body of a person seated on a moving vehicle or owing to self-caused swinging of his body slowly, his mental state becomes calmed. Then he attains *divyaugha* [Wallis: "Divine Flood"] and enjoys the bliss of supernal consciousness." Two independent scholarly translations (Wallis 2017/18 and Singh 1979) now agree on both verse number and content. This technique is no longer flagged.
- **What the verse says:** Sitting on a swing, or in any slowly moving seat, or being gently rocked (by yourself or by another person), settles and stills the emotional state.

## Essence
Slow, rhythmic, self-paced movement — the kind a swing or a rocking chair provides — offers an experience distinct from stillness-based practices. This technique asks the practitioner to notice that experience directly, rather than just enjoying it passively, which is the difference between rocking as a pleasant habit and rocking as a deliberate attention practice.

## Guided Session Script
*(Adaptation — modern secular framing. Approximately 4 minutes. Needs a rocking chair, porch swing, hammock, or a chair you can gently rock in; alternatively, standing and swaying slowly side to side works as a substitute.)*

Settle into a rocking chair, swing, or hammock — or if none is available, stand with your feet hip-width apart, ready to sway gently from side to side.

Begin a slow, easy rocking motion — nothing fast or effortful, just a comfortable back-and-forth or side-to-side rhythm you could sustain without thinking about it.

Close your eyes if that feels stable, or keep a soft, unfocused gaze if you'd rather.

At first, just let the rocking happen and notice the physical sensation of it — the shift of weight, the small motion in your stomach or chest, the rhythm itself.

Now let your attention rest specifically on the *turning points* — the exact moment at each end of the rock where the direction changes, where you're neither quite going one way nor the other yet. There's a brief, almost weightless pause there, similar to the pause at the top or bottom of a breath.

Continue rocking gently, noticing each turning point as it arrives.

Let the rocking slow naturally, on its own, without forcing it to stop. As it slows, notice whether your internal state has shifted at all, and if so, how.

When the rocking has mostly stopped, sit for a few still breaths before opening your eyes.

## Modern Adaptation Notes
- Dropped: "the Divine Flood [of Bliss]" as the named outcome. Replaced with a plain invitation to notice whether anything has shifted, without suggesting what the shift should be or promising any outcome.
- Kept: the core mechanism (slow rhythmic rocking, whether self-initiated or externally provided) essentially as described, generalized from the verse's examples (swing, chariot, elephant) to modern equivalents (rocking chair, hammock, porch swing, or simple standing sway).
- Added (adaptation only): the specific instruction to attend to the turning points of the rock — this is not in the source verse, but is a reasonable extension consistent with the pause/gap logic found elsewhere in the text (compare Technique 01), included here to give the practice a concrete attention-anchor rather than just passive relaxation.
- **QA resolution (2026-09-08):** independent second-translator confirmation (Jaideva Singh, 1979) located and verified — exact match on verse number (83) and content. Flag cleared; see Primary Source section above.
