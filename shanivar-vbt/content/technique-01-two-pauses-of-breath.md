# Technique 01: Two Pauses of Breath

## Primary Source
- **Verse:** Vijñāna-bhairava-tantra, verse 24 (with verse 25 as a direct continuation/clarification of the same practice — the two are often treated as a single dhāraṇā).
- **Translation cited:** Christopher Wallis ("Hareesh"), *A Translation of the Vijñāna-bhairava-tantra* (complete but lacking commentary), self-published 2017, revised 2018, distributed via hareesh.org. Wallis is a Sanskrit scholar (PhD, Oxford) and author of *Tantra Illuminated*; this is his direct verse-by-verse translation from the Sanskrit, not a secondhand paraphrase.
- **Cross-checked against:** A second, independently-worded summary of verse 24 (via Jaideva Singh's *Vijñānabhairava or Divine Consciousness: A Treasury of 112 Types of Yoga*, Motilal Banarsidass, 1979) confirms the same content: attention placed at "the two poles of respiration... where prāṇa [exhale] ends and... apāna [inhale] ends," concentrating on these two points. Wallis's own separate 2022 blog commentary on verses 22–27 (hareesh.org) also independently confirms the verse-24 content and numbering.
- **What the verse says:** The verse describes the breath as the Goddess's own continuous utterance — exhale rising, inhale descending — and instructs the practitioner to pause at "the two places where they arise" and rest there with awareness, in what Wallis translates as a state of "inner fullness." Verse 25 adds: don't turn back from that pause too quickly.

## Essence
This is the closing gap at the top of the inhale and the bottom of the exhale — the two brief, naturally-occurring stillnesses that every breath already contains, whether or not anyone notices them. The technique is not a breathing exercise in the sense of controlling the breath; it's a noticing exercise aimed at the space breath leaves behind. The invitation is to notice whether attention placed in an already-still point feels any different from attention placed on movement.

## Guided Session Script
*(Adaptation — written for a modern, secular guided-audio format. Approximately 4 minutes.)*

Find a position where your spine can be upright without effort — seated is fine, standing is fine. Let your eyes close, or soften your gaze toward the floor a few feet ahead.

For a few breaths, just breathe normally. Don't change anything yet. Notice the air moving in, and the air moving out.

Now bring your attention to the very top of the inhale — that brief moment after the in-breath finishes and before the out-breath begins. There's a small pause there. Most of the time we breathe right through it without noticing. This time, when you reach it, let your attention rest there. Don't hold your breath on purpose — just notice the pause that's already happening.

Breathe out. And now find the other pause — the one at the bottom of the exhale, right before the next inhale starts. Rest your attention there too, for as long as it naturally lasts.

Keep going like this: breathe normally, and each time you arrive at one of the two pauses, let your attention settle into it rather than rushing past it toward the next breath.

If your mind wanders to something else entirely, that's fine — just come back to the next pause, whichever one arrives first.

In the last few breaths, stop trying to find the pauses precisely, and just let your attention rest in the general area of "in between." Notice whatever quality that space has — stillness, spaciousness, or simply the absence of the breath's motion.

When you're ready, let your breathing return to whatever rhythm it wants, and open your eyes.

## Modern Adaptation Notes
- Dropped: the verse's identification of the pause-points with specific subtle-body locations (the heart center for one pause, a point roughly a forearm's length above the head for the other — the *dvādaśānta*) and the implied mantra-syllable pairing (*so'ham*/*haṃsa*) that Wallis's commentary notes as embedded in the Sanskrit verb *uccaret*. These require prior grounding in Śaiva subtle-body cosmology to land correctly and would read as arbitrary mysticism to a first-time secular user; we kept the structurally load-bearing instruction (attend to the two pauses) and dropped the doctrinal scaffolding around *where* in the body to place that attention.
- Dropped: any framing of the result as "the Bhairava-state," "inner fullness as the Goddess," or liberation-adjacent language. Replaced with plain description of what the pause "is" (stillness/spaciousness), consistent with the no-enlightenment-claims constraint.
- Kept: the essential instruction — pause at both ends of the breath cycle, don't force a hold, let awareness rest there rather than immediately moving to the next inhale/exhale. This is the actionable core of both verses 24 and 25.
